# History: the Arcane-Industrial Age

## Status

This is the planned setting baseline. It describes product direction, not a
currently implemented simulation system.

## Before the known peoples

The material universe, its galaxies, and Aether already existed before any
known people arose. Matter gathered into stars and worlds through ordinary
physical processes. Aether was the non-material medium alongside that universe,
able to carry Arcane energy and patterns but neither created by later peoples
nor left behind by their magic.

In this remote age, powerful incorporeal beings entered the known universe from
an order of existence modern scholars call **the Elsewhere**. The Elsewhere is
not a distant region beyond the mapped galaxy. Distance, duration, matter, and
individual identity behave differently there, and no surviving account proves
whether it is another universe, a deeper layer of reality, or something for
which physical language is inadequate.

These visitors were stable arrangements of will, memory, and Aether rather than
material organisms. They could influence the physical universe, but lasting
embodiment required a material **descent anchor**: a constructed site, vessel,
or world-scale pattern that held an incorporeal presence in local reality. They
did not create the universe, Aether, or the peoples who evolved long after
them.

## The powers from the Elsewhere

No record preserves the visitors' own names. Modern scholars group the
surviving symbols, damaged inscriptions, and recurring Aether signatures into
three great alignments. **Order**, **Neutrality**, and **Chaos** describe their
positions on control and change, not good, evil, compassion, or cruelty. Each
alignment contained many orders and sects, and individuals sometimes defected
or cooperated across those boundaries.

| Alignment | Modern coalition name | Guiding belief | Intended fate of the material universe |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Order** | **Lawforged Conclave** | Sacred law, binding covenant, absolute obedience, and impartial judgment | Bring every power and people under one universal covenant whose law stands above preference, circumstance, and ruler alike |
| **Neutrality** | **Veiled Accord** | All conditioned things are impermanent, all phenomena are without self, and nirvana is tranquil | Deny the universe any imposed final form; release attachment to possession, identity, and conflict so that existence may arise and pass in peace |
| **Chaos** | **Myriad Tide** | Spontaneity, natural becoming, freedom from imposed knowledge, and life without fixed judgment | Dissolve artificial cosmic designs and let every being follow its own nature without compulsory law, purpose, or final form |

None maps cleanly to good or evil. The Lawforged Conclave could restrain a
tyrant by placing the same law above ruler and subject, but it could also call a
merciless judgment just because the correct covenant demanded it. Some of its
sects sought to join all minds into one perfectly ordered consciousness. The
Veiled Accord defended material independence, but it did not regard
preservation as an absolute good. Its followers could allow a world, culture,
or even one of their own kind to end rather than bind it permanently or prolong
its suffering through another cycle of war. The Myriad Tide could free life
from imposed form while making stable civilization impossible. Some Tide
spirits were peaceful wanderers and playful makers; others treated every
promise, boundary, and consequence as an illegitimate restraint. Its most
destructive sects used freedom as permission to dissolve any identity or
structure that resisted them.

The Lawforged Conclave taught a body of sacred law known as the **Covenant
Canon**. Modern human scholars compare its emphasis on revealed law, binding
covenant, disciplined interpretation, and consistent judgment with legal and
covenantal traditions found in early Islam and Judaism. The comparison is not
an assertion that either human religion descends from the Conclave or that
their many historical schools hold one identical doctrine.

Three articles stand above the Canon's many judgments:

1. **Law stands above will.** Desire, compassion, rank, and immediate advantage
   cannot make a forbidden act lawful or release a being from a valid duty.
2. **A covenant binds every party.** Subject, ruler, judge, and the power that
   proclaims a law are accountable to the terms they accept; authority without
   obligation is merely domination.
3. **Justice requires consistent judgment.** Like cases must receive like
   judgments, breaches must be answered, and restoration must follow a rule
   known before the dispute rather than the mood of the victor.

The Conclave understood creation as an unfinished covenant. Its spirits
believed that every world, mind, and power needed a defined place, duty, and
protection within one universal law. Descent anchors served not only as means of
embodiment but also as seals of jurisdiction: wherever an anchor stood, the
Canon could be declared, interpreted, and enforced.

This doctrine could create trustworthy agreements, constrain arbitrary power,
and protect those whose rights were written into the covenant. Its danger was
the conviction that correct procedure could sanctify any consequence. Severe
sects punished unavoidable breaches, treated silence as consent, or extended a
covenant to peoples who had never accepted it. Others manipulated exact wording
while violating the law's apparent purpose. Order therefore promised justice
through law without guaranteeing that the law itself was just.

To the Conclave, the Veiled Accord confused release with refusal of duty, while
the Myriad Tide made durable promise and fair judgment impossible. Both were
dangerous because neither accepted a law capable of binding desire across time.

The Veiled Accord taught three definitive marks that later scholars call the
**Veiled Principles**. They resemble Buddhist ideas known to some modern human
traditions, although the Accord reached them in the Elsewhere long before
humanity existed:

1. **All conditioned things are impermanent** (*諸行無常*). Everything that
   arises through causes and conditions will change and eventually pass away.
2. **All phenomena are without self** (*諸法無我*). No form, identity, law,
   world, or spirit possesses an isolated, independent, and eternal self.
3. **Nirvana is tranquil** (*涅槃寂靜*). Peace lies beyond attachment to
   preservation, possession, conquest, identity, and compulsory change.

The Accord therefore rejected both rival absolutes. The Lawforged Conclave
mistook temporary forms for eternal truths and tried to imprison reality inside
them. The Myriad Tide rejected permanent form, yet remained attached to its
ideal of unbounded freedom and spontaneous becoming. To the Accord, Order and
Chaos sustained the same war because each insisted that the universe ought to
follow its preferred way and neither could release its claim upon it.

Most Accord spirits sought to dismantle descent anchors, withdraw from material
possession, and let worlds follow their own causes and consequences. They could
still intervene to prevent domination or reduce suffering, but every
intervention was understood as temporary. More severe sects turned detachment
into indifference, accepted preventable losses too readily, or tried to impose
peace by silencing the desires of others. Neutrality was therefore a discipline
and a contested practice, not automatic wisdom or moral innocence.

The Myriad Tide taught a contrasting set of ideas known as the **Unbound
Ways**:

1. Good and evil, sacred and profane, ruler and subject are judgments made by
   limited minds, not permanent structures of the universe.
2. Knowledge becomes a cage when names, theories, and institutions are treated
   as more real than the changing life they attempt to describe.
3. A being is most free when it acts from its own nature without coercion,
   forced purpose, or fear of an imposed order.

The Tide did not pursue change merely to make everything unstable. It believed
that change becomes destructive when someone tries to command its direction.
A forest growing around a ruin, a traveler abandoning rank, or a community
inventing customs without a sovereign could all express the Unbound Ways. The
Tide admired wandering, improvisation, humor, contradiction, and action that
arose from the moment rather than obedience to an abstract plan.

To the Tide, the Lawforged Conclave mistook its descriptions of reality for
reality itself. The Veiled Accord understood impermanence but risked turning
restraint and release into another disciplined doctrine. Where the Accord
sought freedom by relinquishing its hold on existence, the Tide sought freedom
by living fully within existence without accepting any final authority over
it.

This philosophy did not make every Tide spirit violent or hostile to
cooperation. Voluntary friendship, temporary custom, and freely accepted
responsibility could be natural expressions of life. The danger began when
radical sects declared that every duty was oppression and used liberation as a
justification for betrayal, devastation, or forcing unwanted transformation on
others. Chaos was therefore freedom without a guaranteed moral direction, not
senseless behavior or compulsory cruelty.

## The War of the Unbodied

The visitors first explored the material universe together. They raised descent
anchors around stars, threaded distant systems with resonant routes, and built
structures whose surviving fragments resemble temples, machines, and equations
at the same time. Their alliance failed when they could not agree on whether
material reality should be bound under a universal covenant, released from
every external claim and left to arise and pass on its own, or freed from every
imposed law to follow its spontaneous course.

The resulting **War of the Unbodied** spread across a cosmos in which no known
mortal civilization yet existed. Its combatants turned descent anchors into
weapons, used planets as containment structures, and altered Aether currents to
isolate one another. Some spirits were driven back into the Elsewhere. Others
were divided into incomplete minds, bound into material prisons, or dispersed
as recurring patterns without coherent memory.

The war left physical and Aetheric damage that survives into the present:

- persistent Aether storms and regions where Arcane coupling fails;
- sealed worlds whose geology forms part of an ancient containment pattern;
- systems absent from otherwise consistent gravitational and navigational
  records;
- dormant descent anchors able to give an incorporeal presence material reach;
- fragments that later cultures interpreted as gods, demons, ancestors, or
  stellar monsters; and
- powerful patterns that can be copied as spells or devices without revealing
  the purpose for which they were made.

These remnants do not explain every natural hazard, spell, or modern belief.
Physics, Aether, life, and ordinary Arcane discovery exist independently of the
war.

## The Great Closure and the Long Quiet

The war ended with the **Great Closure**, when the largest descent anchors and
routes to the Elsewhere failed almost simultaneously. One surviving
interpretation claims the Veiled Accord and defectors from both rival
coalitions destroyed their own anchors, releasing their hold on the material
universe to prevent either victory or universal ruin. Other traditions
describe the Closure as a weapon, a natural rejection by the material
universe, or an act performed by an unknown fifth power. No account is
authoritative.

Most surviving visitors were expelled, sealed, or reduced to echoes. Those
still present cannot freely manifest at their former scale; they need a working
anchor, a compatible vessel, or sustained help from within the material
universe. This limitation is why they do not openly rule the modern galaxy and
why recovering an ancient anchor is dangerous even when its apparent function
is useful.

The **Long Quiet** followed. Worlds cooled, ecosystems changed, and the known
peoples eventually evolved through their own histories. Ancient interference
may have altered particular environments or bloodlines, but no modern Race is
the creation, property, moral representative, or predetermined servant of one
Elsewhere coalition.

Much later, the unknown precursor civilization found evidence of the War of the
Unbodied. Some precursor wrecks contain materials and coupling geometries that
resemble descent-anchor fragments, but it remains unknown whether the
precursors merely studied them, reopened one, or were destroyed for unrelated
reasons. Modern Soul Anchors also share limited structural similarities without
proving direct descent from that technology.

Scholars can therefore establish a broad sequence without possessing the whole
truth:

```text
The material universe and Aether
    -> visitors arrive from the Elsewhere
    -> descent anchors and resonant routes spread
    -> the War of the Unbodied
    -> the Great Closure
    -> the Long Quiet
    -> the known peoples arise
    -> the precursor civilization studies ancient remains
    -> the modern peoples enter the wider galaxy
```

## Present era

Spelljammer takes place in an advanced **Arcane-Industrial Age**. Magic is real,
trained, and widely used beside mature spacefaring technology. Civilizations
are not medieval worlds with a few exotic artifacts: they build shipyards,
sealed habitats, navigation networks, factories, sensors, weapons, and
interstellar trade routes.

Industrial technology begins in a rugged **dieselpunk** era of compression
engines, generators, armored hulls, analog controls, propellant drives, and
shell cannon. Its advanced frontier is **atompunk**: reactors, radiothermal
power, nuclear-thermal propulsion, shielded installations, and high-energy
industry. The latest atompunk equipment includes powered armor, laser weapons,
and plasma weapons. These terms describe the setting's functional technology level, not
a requirement to simulate every pipe, fuel mixture, or reactor detail.

## Arcane and Aether

Humans, elves, and dwarves originated on different homeworlds, separated before
the opening of space. Their later diaspora, mixed ports, and shared ship crews
do not erase those independent planetary histories. No homeworld determines an
individual's beliefs, profession, or political allegiance.

**Arcane** is the umbrella term for magic: its learned traditions, spells,
enchantments, wards, and magical devices. **Aether** is a non-material medium
permeating space that carries Arcane energy and patterns. It is not a substance
that can be mined, bottled, or carried as cargo. Its currents, density, and
interference can still be observed and can affect Arcane work.

The decisive discovery of the age was that Aether exists beyond a world's
atmosphere. Elven historical records describe the earliest successful
spaceflight: resonance navigators sensed open-space Aether and guided a
starwood sailcraft along it. Before this, Arcane practice was local and ships
relied on conventional propulsion.

Generations later, dwarf and gnome researchers independently measured Aether
currents, interference, resonance, and charge with repeatable instruments and
experiments. Their scientific coupling methods made Aether control teachable,
repairable, and usable in ship equipment instead of relying only on inherited
navigation traditions. This opened broader Arcane trade routes, wreck
expeditions, and interstellar settlements; it did not make Industrial
propulsion obsolete.

## The First Concord

During an early joint elven-dwarven expedition, explorers reached the human
homeworld. Humanity had not yet entered a spacefaring age: its societies were
roughly between their medieval and renaissance periods, with ocean-going
navigation, workshops, early scientific inquiry, and competing states, but no
means to cross the void. First contact was therefore not a discovery of an
empty frontier, but a meeting between established starfarers and a populous,
independent world.

The first lasting agreement, later called the **First Concord**, joined elven,
dwarven, and human communities. Elves shared Aether-route lore, resonance
navigation, and the caution needed to travel through unfamiliar currents.
Dwarves shared instruments, engineering practices, shipyard methods, and ways
to make Aether-coupled systems repairable. Humans chose what to adopt, trained
their own navigators and engineers, and adapted the new knowledge to their
own ports, institutions, and ambitions. The alliance helped humanity launch
its first ships; it did not make humans a client people or erase their older
cultures.

The Concord is remembered differently across the galaxy: as a hopeful founding
alliance, a source of unequal early access to space, or a bargain that opened
too much too quickly. Its legacy explains why human crews are now common on
interstellar routes, why mixed settlements retain both resonance and
industrial craft, and why control of teaching, shipbuilding, and first-contact
records can still be politically contentious.

## The Precursor Wrecks

Orc and goblin societies reached space by a separate path. On their shared
ancestral homeworld, they uncovered the buried ships of an unknown civilization
whose voyages predate the earliest elven records by an unmeasured span. The
wrecks could no longer fly, but their hulls, star maps, and ruined Aether
couplers proved that the void had been crossed long before any living culture's
known history.

Generations of orc and goblin salvagers, artisans, and scholars studied those
remains. They could not reproduce the precursor vessels whole; instead, they
reverse-engineered the few principles they could test: how a physical device
couples to Aether, how to survive a void crossing, and how to repair a ship
with limited materials. Their first spacecraft were rugged, incomplete
interpretations of ancient designs. Later fleets developed distinct orc and
goblin solutions rather than remaining copies of dead technology.

This origin gives their spacefaring powers a claim to knowledge the First
Concord does not control. Orc expansion is openly conquest-driven: their war
fleets seek territory, tribute, and command of strategic routes by force.
Their conflict with elven, dwarven, and human states also turns on wreck sites,
access to Aether technology, and competing accounts of the past. Goblin groups
may support those fleets, profit from them, or pursue their own interests. The
precursor civilization remains unknown; its legacy is a source of exploration,
danger, and disagreement, not an authority that explains every modern system.

Other peoples entered the wider galaxy through their own histories: gnome and
dwarf researchers made Aether coupling measurable; Somnari followed recurring
psychic impressions toward distant signals; the gothic Veyr, descendants of an
ancient empire broken by civil war and stellar disaster, built refuge and trade
routes around their fragmentary past; Eidolons began as survivors of a Soul
Anchor evacuation; and Tharun reclaimed Veyr colonial shipyards after the
empire enslaved them and stripped their world of resources, turning planetary
trail-reading into star-route navigation. That legacy fuels enduring
Veyr-Tharun hostility. Half-elves arose in the mixed settlements of the First
Concord rather than from a separate ancient state. See [`Races.md`](Races.md)
for these histories and their individual limits.

A caster uses the surrounding Aether as a focus medium, while a ship couples to
it through physical resonators, conduits, and accumulators. **Aether charge**
is not stored Aether; it is the usable Arcane energy that a device has captured
and held after coupling to the medium. Ordinary personal casting keeps this
simple: Focus represents the caster's controlled use of available Arcane
energy. A spell or item names Aether charge only when that extra cost is an
interesting player decision.

## Two coexisting traditions

| Tradition | Everyday role | Characteristic feel |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Arcane | Spells, enchantment, wards, magical navigation, and devices powered through Aether | Strange, flexible, and dependent on knowledge, Aether charge, and safe use |
| Industrial | Engines, reactors, armor, physical ammunition, fabrication, sensors, and heavy propulsion | Rugged, powerful, and dependent on fuel, parts, space, and maintenance |

Neither tradition is inherently superior or tied to a Race, morality, or one
faction. A port may use runic beacons beside diesel cranes; an industrial ship
can carry an Arcane ward; an Arcane vessel still needs a physical hull, cargo,
crew, and repairs. Hybrid builds are possible when their loadout pays the
visible slot and resource cost.

## Craft traditions

Elven and dwarven craft are both renowned, but their goals are different.
Elven **resonance craft** forges or grows a small number of long-lived objects
whose material, bearer, and nearby Aether are tuned together. Its weapons,
armor, starwood hulls, wards, and navigation fittings are elegant masterworks
that reward careful use and retuning. Dwarven **industrial craft** favors
repeatable alloys, interchangeable parts, documented standards, powerful
engines, and equipment that can be repaired or upgraded in a shipyard.

Neither is a racial lock or a universal quality ranking. A dwarf can commission
a resonance blade, an elf can wear powered armor, and mixed crews can combine
both traditions. The distinction creates different equipment and ship choices,
not a judgment about which people are more capable.

This difference also shapes exploration. Many elven expeditions preserve and
map extraordinary vistas, nebulae, Aether tides, and living worlds. Many
dwarven expeditions survey asteroids, comet fields, moons, and gas giants for
rare ore, industrial gases, reactor material, and defensible extraction sites.
Both perspectives make the same galaxy valuable in different ways.

## Everyday life and conflict

The setting should feel lived-in. Magic and advanced machinery shape work,
commerce, medicine, warfare, exploration, language, and law. They also create
different opportunities: a ward can protect a hatch, a reactor can power a
station, an enchantment can preserve a tool, a powered suit can protect a
boarder, and a laser or plasma weapon can challenge armor. None is a universal
answer.

The game presents these ideas through readable choices rather than technical
simulation. A player sees useful values and consequences—such as Focus, Aether
charge,
energy, fuel, ammunition, armor, shields, and module slots—not hidden
engineering calculations.

## Design boundaries

- Magic requires training or an explicit Racial Perk; it is not a substitute
  for every skill or social decision.
- Atompunk technology is advanced but controlled: nuclear weapons, radiation,
  and reactor disasters appear only as authored high-consequence content, not
  routine background bookkeeping.
- The setting remains original. It does not import lore, technology, names, or
  cosmology from another game or fictional universe.
